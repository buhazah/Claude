# Phoenix — Charter

What this company is, independent of how it is built.

Everything in `docs/` describes an implementation and will be wrong eventually.
This document should not be. Written to be read in ten years by someone deciding
whether a proposal belongs here.

---

## The business we are in

> **We take responsibility for a company's customer acquisition, and we make
> ourselves checkable.**

Businesses that buy customers face a permanent conflict: the numbers describing
whether it worked are supplied by the party being paid. The platform reports its
own performance. The agency reports its own performance. The advertiser
adjudicates between two interested parties using data neither of them can
independently verify, while spending a substantial share of their revenue.

That conflict is structural, not technological. Better tools have not removed it
in twenty years and will not remove it in twenty more, because it is a conflict
of interest rather than a measurement problem. Auditors were not disrupted by
better accounting software.

**Phoenix exists on the advertiser's side of that conflict.** We operate the
acquisition function *and* we measure it against a source the client controls.
Being both operator and honest scorekeeper is uncomfortable, and it is the
position that has value.

Everything else — the technology, the channels, the methods — is
implementation.

## 1. Who we serve

Not a vertical. A structural situation:

**A business where buying customers is a top-three cost line, run by someone who
can decide, using money they can feel.**

Five conditions, all required:

| Condition | Why it is non-negotiable |
|---|---|
| **A purchase is a discrete, recordable event** | If we cannot tell whether it worked, we cannot be accountable, and accountability is the product |
| **A source of revenue truth we do not control** | Their books, their payment records. Without an independent measure we are just another interested party reporting on ourselves |
| **Acquisition spend large enough to matter, small enough to hurt** | Below that, nobody needs us; above it, they hire a team |
| **One person feels the pain and can sign** | Committees buy software. People hand over problems |
| **They have been disappointed before** | The most reliable qualifier. Someone who has been burned already believes the problem exists; educating a happy buyer is the most expensive sale there is |

### Who we turn away, and mean it

- Businesses whose outcome we cannot measure. **We decline revenue on this basis.**
  Taking money to optimise something unmeasurable is how an operator becomes a
  storyteller, and it takes about two quarters.
- Anyone who wants us to hold their accounts, their billing, or their money.
- Anyone shopping on price. A buyer who chose us because we were cheapest will
  leave for the same reason, having learned nothing and taught us nothing.
- Anyone who wants the appearance of rigour. We produce numbers that are
  sometimes unflattering, and a client who needs a good story will eventually ask
  us to become one.
- Categories where the honest version of our work would be harmful regardless of
  how well it performed.

**The last three are refusals of revenue that would have been profitable.** A
company defined only by what it accepts is defined by whoever is buying.

## 2. The problem they pay us to solve

The surface request is *"get me more customers cheaper."* That is not the pain.

The pain is that they are spending a great deal of money on something they
**cannot verify, cannot safely delegate, and cannot stop.**

- **Cannot verify** — the performance report comes from the party being paid.
- **Cannot safely delegate** — handing it over means losing sight of it; keeping
  it means it consumes the person least able to spare the time.
- **Cannot stop** — switching off acquisition ends the business, so there is no
  option to walk away while they think about it.

That is a trapped position, and it is why the emotional register of this purchase
is relief rather than enthusiasm.

**What we sell is the ability to stop thinking about it without being reckless.**

Three things have to be simultaneously true for that, and no single existing
option delivers all three: the work is done well, the work is *legible*, and the
authority under which it happens is bounded. An in-house hire gives legibility
and bounded authority without depth. An agency gives depth without legibility. A
tool gives legibility while handing the work back.

## 3. Why they keep paying every month

Ranked by how durable each reason is, not by how good it sounds:

**1. They can check us against their own records.** Every number traces to
something they can verify without asking us. This is the rarest property in the
category and the hardest to copy, because it requires being willing to publish
figures that make us look worse than our competitors' figures make them look.

**2. They know what happened and why, without asking.** The most common complaint
about the incumbent option is not poor performance — it is *"I don't know what
they do."* Being legible every week is cheap to deliver and enormously
underestimated.

**3. Leaving means rebuilding a year of context.** What we have learned about
their margins, their buyers, their brand, what has been tried and failed. This is
real switching cost and it must remain **honest**: everything is exportable on
request, and the cost of leaving is rebuilding the *learning*, never the
retrieval of their own data. A company that retains customers by making exit
technically difficult has stopped competing.

**4. The results.** Fourth, deliberately. Results are noisy month to month and
partly outside anyone's control. A business that retains only on last month's
numbers is a business that churns on a bad quarter that was not its fault.

**5. Someone is accountable and answers.** Not a queue, not a dashboard. A name.

**Stated honestly:** this ranking is a hypothesis. It is being tested directly on
the first cohort of clients (`docs/10-VALIDATION.md`), including the possibility
that the cheapest thing we do is the thing they would be most upset to lose. If
the evidence contradicts the ranking, the ranking changes — the *principle* that
retention must be earned monthly does not.

## 4. What we refuse to build

Refusals define a company more precisely than features do. These are permanent.

**We will not become the advertiser of record.** The accounts, the platform
relationship, and the policy liability stay with the client. We operate inside
what they own, under permissions they grant and can revoke without asking us.

**We will not be paid a share of what we spend.** The conventional model pays the
operator more for spending more, which is an incentive pointed exactly backwards.
We charge a fee for the work. When the right answer is *"spend less,"* nothing in
our compensation argues with it.

**We will not report a number the client cannot reconcile.** If a figure cannot
be traced to something they can check, it does not appear in a report — however
flattering, however standard in the industry.

**We will not let anything act outside an authority granted in advance, in
writing, with a limit and an expiry.** Not because automation is dangerous, but
because unbounded authority is.

**We will not make leaving difficult.** No exit fees, no data hostage-taking, no
annual lock-in. Everything exportable on request. We intend to be fired in a
month if we have not earned the month.

**We will not keep learning from a client who has left.** Whatever we learned
from them goes with them, and we build only in ways that make that mechanically
possible rather than contractually promised.

**We will not sell attention as though it were outcome.** Impressions, clicks,
engagement and reach are diagnostics. Presenting them as results is the oldest
misdirection in the category.

**We will not build the dashboard as the product.** A dashboard returns the work
to the person who hired us to take it away.

**We will not take work whose success we cannot measure.** See §1.

## 5. Principles

Seven. Each has a test you can apply to a proposal, and a cost — a principle that
costs nothing is a preference.

**1 · Everything we claim, the client can check.**
*Test:* could they verify this from records we do not control?
*Cost:* our reported results look worse than competitors who use flattering
sources. We lose deals to better numbers that are less true.

**2 · Authority is granted, bounded, and expires.**
*Test:* what is the limit, who set it, when does it lapse, how is it revoked?
*Cost:* more friction than a competitor who asks once and assumes forever.

**3 · Judgment proposes; rules decide.**
Whatever produces the judgment — a person, a model, something not yet invented —
the thing standing between it and a client's money is deterministic, inspectable
and testable.
*Cost:* we forgo capability that only exists in unbounded systems.

**4 · Say plainly what we do not know.**
Unmeasurable things are labelled unmeasurable. Estimates are labelled estimates
and carry their uncertainty.
*Cost:* less confident than the competition in every meeting.

**5 · Authority is earned with evidence, never asserted.**
Nothing gets more autonomy because it seems reliable. It gets more because it was
right, measurably, over a period, at that specific thing.
*Cost:* slow. Every new capability serves a probationary period.

**6 · The boring parts are the product.**
Not overspending, not causing an account loss, not reporting a wrong figure, not
missing the week. Unglamorous, and it is what people pay for after the second
month.
*Cost:* engineering attention goes to reliability rather than to the things that
demo well.

**7 · Prefer the mechanism that does not require trusting us.**
Given a choice between a policy and a mechanism, choose the mechanism. Between a
promise and a limit, choose the limit.
*Cost:* mechanisms are more work to build and less flexible afterwards. That is
the point.

## 6. What success means

### The four that matter

| Metric | Why it is the real verdict |
|---|---|
| **Net revenue retention** | Whether people keep paying, and pay more. Everything else is a leading indicator of this |
| **Renewal at a raised price** | The only unambiguous signal in commerce. Not satisfaction, not a survey — money, again, more of it, voluntarily |
| **Verified acquisition cost against their own baseline** | Measured from *their* records, not ours. If this does not move, we are being paid for reassurance |
| **Hours per client, flat against account size** | Whether this is a company or a job. Rising hours with account size caps us at whatever the founder can personally watch |

### The three that must be zero

Not averaged, not trended, not traded against anything:

- **Actions taken outside a granted authority.**
- **Reported figures that could not be reconciled.**
- **Client account losses we caused.**

A single instance of any of these is an incident with a name attached, not a
percentage in a report.

### What we refuse to celebrate

Volume of work produced. Sophistication of method. Technology adopted. Headcount.
Revenue growth without retention. Each is a way of feeling successful while the
question in §3 goes unanswered.

## 7. What must remain true

The constitution. If a proposal violates one of these, the proposal is wrong —
not the clause.

1. **The client owns their accounts, their data, and their relationship with the
   platform.** We operate inside what is theirs.
2. **Every figure we report traces to a source the client can check
   independently.**
3. **Nothing acts outside an authority the client granted in advance, with a
   limit and an expiry, revocable immediately.**
4. **We are paid a fee for work, never a share of what we spend.**
5. **What we learn from a client leaves when they leave.**
6. **We state what we do not know.**
7. **A named human is accountable for what the system does.** Automation never
   dilutes this; it concentrates it.
8. **We can be fired in a month.** No agreement outlives the client's willingness
   to keep paying.

Eight clauses. None mentions a technology, a channel, or a method, and none would
need rewriting if every one of those changed tomorrow.

## Why this survives the technology

The obvious challenge: *if machines get good enough, doesn't this become
unnecessary?*

Consider what improves and what does not.

**What technology improves:** the quality of the work, its cost, its speed, its
sophistication. All of it, for us and for every competitor, on the same schedule.
None of it is a durable position.

**What technology does not touch:**

- **The conflict of interest.** The platform reporting its own performance is not
  a technical limitation. Better measurement inside the platform is still
  measurement by an interested party.
- **The need for bounded authority.** More capable automation makes bounded
  authority *more* necessary, not less. The blast radius grows with the
  capability.
- **The need for someone accountable.** Capability does not create
  accountability. Accountability is a relationship between people, and a more
  autonomous system increases the demand for a name attached to it.
- **The trapped position in §2.** A business that cannot verify, cannot safely
  delegate and cannot stop is in that position because of structure, not
  technology.

So the durable company is not *"the one with the best automation."* It is **the
one positioned on the advertiser's side of a conflict that does not go away,
operating under authority that is explicitly bounded, and reporting numbers the
client can verify without trusting us.**

Today, the best way to do that work happens to involve AI. That is a fact about
this decade, not about this company. When the tools change, everything in `docs/`
gets rewritten and nothing in this document does.

**The test for any proposal, permanently:**

> Does it make us more useful, more checkable, or more accountable to the person
> paying us?
>
> If it only makes us more capable, it is not yet a reason to build it.

## Amending this document

Harder to change than anything else here, deliberately.

**§7 changes only if a clause is shown to be actively harmful to clients** — not
inconvenient, not commercially costly, not slowing us down. Those are the
expected costs of the clauses, not arguments against them.

**§4 and §5 change only with a written argument for why the refusal or the
principle was wrong from the beginning**, kept in the repository alongside the
change so the reasoning survives the person who made it.

**§1, §2, §3 and §6 are expected to change**, as evidence from real clients
arrives. They are claims about the world, and the world answers.

The distinction is the whole point: the first group is who we are, the second is
what we currently believe. Confusing them in either direction is how a company
either ossifies or drifts.
